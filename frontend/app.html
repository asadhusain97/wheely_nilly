How the numbers work

Glossary

Plain-English definitions for the options and portfolio numbers in Wheely Nilly. Examples are simplified and exclude taxes. "Good" ranges are app starting points, not promises or trade recommendations.

Definitions for portfolio performance, capital, contracts, strategy settings, and Radar terms.

Options basics

Option contract Call or put
A contract tied to an underlying stock or fund. A call buyer gets the right to buy at the strike; a put buyer gets the right to sell. The seller takes the other side of that obligation. Standard equity contracts usually cover 100 shares, but adjusted contracts can differ.
Example: one $50 call usually controls 100 shares at a $50 strike.
Contract value = option price per share × contract multiplier
Option contract on Wikipedia
Covered call CC
You own shares and sell a call against them. You collect premium now, but assignment can force you to sell the shares at the strike. The premium gives only a small downside cushion and the call caps the stock's upside above the strike.
Example: own 100 shares, sell one $55 call for $2. If assigned, the effective sale price is $57 per share before fees.
Effective sale price = strike + net premium per share
Cash-secured put CSP
You sell a put and reserve enough cash to buy the shares if assigned. You keep the premium, but a large stock drop can leave you buying well above the market price.
Example: sell one $50 put for $2 and reserve $5,000. If assigned, the effective purchase price is $48 per share before fees.
Breakeven at expiration = strike − net premium per share
Premium Option price
The option's quoted per-share price. A buyer pays it; a seller receives it. For a standard 100-share contract, multiply the quote by 100, then include fees for the actual cash flow.
Example: a $1.25 quote is $125 for one standard contract before fees.
Gross premium = option price × multiplier × contracts
ITM / ATM / OTM Moneyness state
In the money means exercising now has value. Calls are ITM above the strike; puts are ITM below it. At the money means stock and strike are about equal. Out of the money means no intrinsic value. ITM short options have greater assignment risk, especially near expiration, but assignment can occur earlier.
Example: with stock at $100, a $95 call is ITM and a $95 put is OTM.
Moneyness on Wikipedia
Intrinsic & extrinsic value What premium contains
Intrinsic value is what exercise would be worth now. Extrinsic value is the rest of the premium, paid for remaining time and uncertainty. Extrinsic value generally shrinks toward zero by expiration.
Example: stock at $105 and a $100 call priced at $7 has $5 intrinsic value and $2 extrinsic value.
Call intrinsic = max(stock − strike, 0)
Put intrinsic = max(strike − stock, 0)
Extrinsic value = option price − intrinsic value
Exercise & assignment
Exercise is the buyer using the contract. Assignment is the seller being required to fulfill it. A short call assignment sells shares; a short put assignment buys shares. Do not assume an OTM option cannot be assigned or that every ITM option will be assigned.
Close a short option before the market close if you must remove that day's assignment exposure. Broker deadlines and contract terms still apply.

Strategy settings

Goals & ticker changes Settings layers
A goal supplies the full monitoring profile for its strategy. A ticker change replaces one value only for that ticker and strategy.
Effective value = ticker change, otherwise goal value
Goal profiles Keep Shares · Earn Income · Plan Exit · Plan Entry
Editable starting points for different intentions. “Aggressive” means committed to that goal, not blindly chasing the largest premium.
Keep Shares: 30–60 DTE, 105%–125% moneyness, 0.08–0.18 delta, and a 35% close target. Earn Income: 14–35 DTE, 0.30–0.45 delta, at least 1% period return, and a 50% close target.
Plan Exit: 7–21 DTE, 95%–105% moneyness, 0.45–0.65 delta, and a 90% close target. Plan Entry: 7–28 DTE, 97%–100% moneyness, 0.40–0.55 delta, and an 85% close target.
Plan Exit admits a below-spot call only when the ticker has a minimum net sale-price guard. The guard remains the real sell or buy boundary for that ticker.
Covered calls at the Options Industry Council
Cash-secured puts at the Options Industry Council
DTE range Time window
The allowed number of calendar days until expiration. More time usually means more premium and more time to react. Less time means faster time decay, but price sensitivity and assignment pressure can rise sharply near expiration.
Goal ranges are Keep Shares 30–60, Earn Income 14–35, Plan Exit 7–21, and Plan Entry 7–28. Radar favors the midpoint of the saved range, so each goal controls its own ranking target. These are starting points, not a universal best window.
Minimum DTE ≤ days to expiration ≤ maximum DTE
Moneyness Strike / stock
Strike price compared with the stock price. It locates the strike without changing when you switch between a call and a put.
Example: a $95 strike on a $100 stock is 95%. That strike is OTM for a put and ITM for a call.
100% is at the money. Farther from 100% usually means less premium and less immediate assignment pressure for an OTM option. The direction depends on call versus put.
Moneyness = strike price ÷ stock price × 100%
Moneyness on Wikipedia
Target delta range Absolute delta
Keeps contracts inside your chosen delta sensitivity. Wheely Nilly compares absolute values, so a −0.25 put counts as 0.25. A blank minimum or maximum leaves that side open.
Goal ranges are Keep Shares 0.08–0.18, Earn Income 0.30–0.45, Plan Exit 0.45–0.65, and Plan Entry 0.40–0.55. Radar favors each saved range's midpoint. Higher absolute delta generally means more premium and more stock-like risk, not guaranteed assignment odds.
Minimum delta ≤ |option delta| ≤ maximum delta
Maximum spread Liquidity rule
The widest bid-ask gap Radar will accept, measured against the midpoint. A tighter spread usually means lower trading friction and a midpoint estimate you can trust more.
Example: $1.90 bid and $2.10 ask gives a $2 midpoint and a 10% spread.
Radar labels ≤2% excellent, ≤5% good, ≤10% fair, and warns above 15%. Keep Shares and Earn Income cap eligibility at 8%; Exit and Entry use 10% so the target strike has slightly more room. A wide spread is a cost warning even when activity looks healthy.
Midpoint = (bid + ask) ÷ 2
Spread % = (ask − bid) ÷ midpoint × 100%
Minimum open interest Liquidity rule
The fewest outstanding contracts a candidate may have. It is a rough activity check, not a promise that your order will fill. Current bid and ask prices matter more to execution.
Radar's product guide calls 2,000+ excellent, 500+ good, 100+ fair, and below 100 poor. Keep Shares and Earn Income require 100; Exit and Entry require 50 to preserve more target-price choices. The private system fallback of 10 is only a permissive screen.
Passes when open interest ≥ minimum
Open interest on Wikipedia
Minimum volume Liquidity rule
The fewest contracts that must have traded today. Volume resets each session, so a zero early in the day is not the same signal as a zero near the close.
Radar's product guide calls 500+ excellent, 100+ good, 20+ fair, and below 20 poor. Keep Shares and Earn Income require 20; Exit and Entry require 10. Always read volume with the spread because volume resets each session.
Passes when today's volume ≥ minimum
Minimum return One option period
The lowest estimated return Radar will accept for this contract's own lifetime. It uses net credit after the estimated fee and does not annualize the result.
Example: $100 net credit on $4,900 of put capital is about 2.04% for the contract term.
There is no universal good minimum. Higher return usually pays for some mix of higher volatility, closer strike, longer time, or worse liquidity. Compare the same strategy, ticker, and DTE before calling one return better.
The goal floors are Keep Shares 0.20%, Earn Income 1.00%, Plan Exit 0.25%, and Plan Entry 0.50% for the option period. A candidate below the floor is excluded, not merely ranked lower.
Covered call = net premium ÷ current value of 100 shares
Cash-secured put = net premium ÷ (strike × 100 − net premium)
Net price guard Ticker setting
Your ticker-specific line in the sand. For a call, it rejects an effective sale price below your floor. For a put, it rejects an effective purchase price above your ceiling. It filters candidates; it never places an order.
A useful guard comes from the price at which you are genuinely willing to sell or buy the shares. A richer premium should not rescue an unacceptable strike.
Covered-call net sale price = strike + net premium per share
Put breakeven price = strike − net premium per share

Performance

Booked profit Booked P&L
Realized option profit or loss from matched contracts that have closed, after recorded fees. An open credit is cash received, but Wheely Nilly does not count it as booked profit yet.
Example: collect $200 net, then pay $80 net to close. Booked profit is $120.
Positive made money; negative lost money. It excludes any unrealized stock gain or loss, so it is not the wheel's total economic return.
Opening net credit + closing net cash flow
Return on collateral Return
Booked option profit for qualified closed contracts as a percentage of the capital committed when each trade opened. Trades with missing profit, collateral, or holding time are excluded.
Example: $120 profit on $4,800 collateral is a 2.5% return.
Positive is profitable and negative is a loss. Compare it with holding time and risk. A higher percentage is not automatically better if it came from a closer strike or a more volatile stock.
Qualified booked profit ÷ qualified entry collateral
Annualized return Annualized rate
A simple one-year pace calculated from a shorter result. It makes different holding periods easier to compare, but it does not mean the return can be repeated for a year. Very short trades can produce eye-catching, unstable annualized numbers.
Example: 2% over 30 days becomes about 24.3% annualized: 2% × 365 ÷ 30.
Use it as a comparison rate, not a forecast. Check the raw period return, DTE, losses, and excluded trades before judging it.
Dashboard: qualified booked profit × 365 ÷ Σ(collateral × days held)
Radar: net premium ÷ return collateral × 365 ÷ DTE
Remaining annualized return
The yearly pace of the option's remaining time value relative to current capital at risk. It asks, "How much annualized time value remains if the inputs stayed unchanged?" It is not a prediction or the Close signal.
Higher means more time value remains to earn, but usually with more time or market risk still attached. Compare it with premium already captured and days left.
Remaining extrinsic ÷ capital at risk × 365 ÷ max(DTE, 1)
Capital velocity
Dollars of booked option profit produced by each $1,000 of collateral at a 30-day pace. It puts dollars, capital, and time into one realized-efficiency number.
Example: $200 profit from $10,000 held for 30 days equals $20 per $1,000 per 30 days.
Above $0 means profitable; below $0 means losses. Higher is more efficient only when the underlying risk and missing-trade count are comparable.
Qualified booked profit ÷ collateral-days × 30 × $1,000
Premium capture
How much of the opening credit has become estimated or booked profit. For an open contract, Radar subtracts an ask-price buyback and estimated closing fee. The dashboard uses closed contracts.
Example: collect $200 and estimate an $80 buyback. Profit if closed is $120, so capture is 60%.
0% means no premium retained, 50% means half, and 100% means the full credit. A negative number means an estimated or booked loss. Goal targets are Keep Shares 35%, Earn Income 50%, Plan Exit 90%, and Plan Entry 85%; your saved setting controls the signal.
Open contract = estimated profit if closed ÷ opening net credit
Dashboard = booked profit ÷ positive premium received on closed contracts
Monthly booked P&L
Booked profit and loss grouped by the month each contract closed, then stacked by ticker.

Capital & contracts

Wheel capital
Capital currently tied to the wheel: cash securing open puts plus broker cost basis for complete share lots. It is exposure, not profit and not current market value.
There is no ideal dollar amount. Judge it against account size, diversification, and how much stock you could be assigned. More deployed capital raises concentration even when option income rises.
Open CSP collateral + share capital
CSP collateral Cash secured
Cash set aside to buy the shares if a short put is assigned. Wheely Nilly shows the full strike obligation and does not subtract premium here.
Example: one standard $50 put requires $5,000 of gross collateral.
Strike × contract multiplier × open contracts
Share capital Shares committed
Broker-reported cost basis for complete share lots. For one covered call, only the shares committed to that call count as its collateral.
Example: 100 shares with a $48 broker basis use $4,800 of share capital.
Broker cost basis per share × 100-share lots
Collateral Ticker view
Current CSP collateral plus share capital for a ticker. On an individual contract, it means the cash securing a put or the cost basis of shares committed to a call.
Open CSPs / open CCs
The number of current short put or short call contracts. CC means covered call; one standard contract represents 100 shares unless its multiplier says otherwise.
Contract multiplier
How many shares one contract controls. Standard equity options usually use 100. Corporate actions can create adjusted contracts with a different deliverable, so check the recorded multiplier.
Total shares controlled = multiplier × contracts
Premium received
Net cash credited when the short option opened, after recorded fees and allocated to the matched contract quantity. It becomes booked profit only as the contract closes.
Gross opening credit − recorded opening fees
Buyback debit
A conservative estimate of what it would cost to close the short option now. Wheely Nilly uses the displayed ask plus estimated closing fees. A limit order may fill better, but no fill is guaranteed.
Ask per share × multiplier × contracts + estimated closing fees
Profit if closed
Estimated option profit if you bought the contract back now at the displayed ask. It can change with every quote and becomes booked only after an actual close.
Positive means an estimated option profit; negative means an estimated option loss. It excludes unrealized stock gain or loss.
Opening net credit − buyback debit
Earned per day
The current estimated option profit divided by calendar days held. It describes the pace so far, not what tomorrow will earn. A same-day position counts as one day.
Profit if closed ÷ days held
Extrinsic per day
Remaining time value divided evenly across calendar days left. Actual theta decay is not even and can change with stock price and IV. An option expiring today uses one day.
Compare this with earned per day. A small remaining amount may make closing attractive, but assignment risk, taxes, fees, and your strategy still matter.
Remaining extrinsic value ÷ max(DTE, 1)
Closing cash flow Close cost
The net cash from closing a contract. A buy-to-close cost is negative; expiration and assignment contribute zero closing option cash in booked option profit.
DTE Days to expiration
Calendar days until expiration. Zero means the contract expires today. "Next expiry" is the earliest expiration among your current short options.
Lower is not simply better. Time decay tends to accelerate near expiration, while short-dated ATM options also become more sensitive to stock moves.
Stock price
The latest available brokerage quote, falling back to the refreshed position price when a quote is unavailable.

Radar opportunities

Candidate rank
The order of contracts that passed every saved rule. It is a fit ranking, not a prediction of profit. Contracts with delta data come first. If both delta bounds exist, Radar next favors their midpoint, then the DTE midpoint, period return, spread, expiration, and strike.
Rank 1 means "closest to your settings" among the current matches. It does not mean safe, underpriced, or most likely to profit.
Strike price
The contract's fixed per-share transaction price. A call holder can buy at the strike; a put holder can sell at the strike. A short-option seller takes the opposite obligation if assigned.
For wheel trades, choose a call strike where you would accept selling and a put strike where you would accept buying. Premium cannot make a bad stock price safe.
Underlying price
The market-data provider's stock or fund price captured with the option chain and used for Radar calculations.
Executable option price Midpoint · Bid · Ask
The per-share price used for an estimate. Bid is the best displayed buying price; ask is the best displayed selling price. Radar uses midpoint only when the spread passes your limit. Close uses ask for a conservative buyback estimate.
Example: $1.90 bid and $2.10 ask has a $2 midpoint. A $2 limit order might fill, but midpoint is not a guaranteed execution.
Midpoint = (bid + ask) ÷ 2
Bid-ask spread Quote width
The gap between the best displayed ask and bid, measured against midpoint. It is a direct warning about trading friction. Tighter is better, all else equal.
Radar's guide: ≤2% excellent, ≤5% good, ≤10% fair, and above 15% a hard warning. Cheap options can show large percentages from a small cent gap, so inspect dollars too.
Spread % = (ask − bid) ÷ midpoint × 100%
Bid-ask spread on Wikipedia
Open interest / volume Liquidity activity
Open interest is outstanding contracts after prior opens and closes. Volume is contracts traded today and resets each session. Neither tells you whether trades were bullish or guarantees a fill.
Higher usually supports liquidity, but the live spread is the clearest execution check. Radar scores open interest of 100 / 500 / 2,000 as fair / good / excellent and volume of 20 / 100 / 500 the same way.
Net contract credit
Estimated cash left from selling one standard contract after Radar's fee estimate.
Example: a $2 midpoint produces $200 gross; after a $0.65 estimated fee, net contract credit is $199.35.
Net contract credit = executable option price × 100 − estimated fee
Return on capital Period return · Contract term
Estimated net credit as a percentage of the capital backing the candidate for its own DTE. It is a term return, not annualized and not guaranteed.
Compare candidates with similar ticker risk, strike, and DTE. A larger number often comes with a closer strike, higher IV, or weaker liquidity.
Covered call = net contract credit ÷ current value of 100 shares
Cash-secured put = net contract credit ÷ (strike × 100 − net contract credit)
Net sale / breakeven price
The stock transaction price after spreading estimated net credit over 100 shares. Calls show what you would effectively receive if assigned; puts show what you would effectively pay.
Example: a $50 strike with $199.35 net credit gives a $51.99 call sale price or a $48.01 put breakeven, rounded.
Net sale price = strike + net contract credit ÷ 100
Breakeven price = strike − net contract credit ÷ 100
Delta
Estimated change in the option's per-share price for a $1 stock move, with other model inputs held still. Calls usually range from 0 to 1; puts from −1 to 0. Delta changes as price, time, and IV change.
Example: a 0.25 delta option may move about $0.50 for a $2 stock move at that moment. It will not stay exactly 0.25.
Wheely Nilly ranks the midpoint of each goal's saved delta range highest. Traders sometimes use absolute delta as a rough chance-of-finishing-ITM proxy, but it is not the real-world probability of assignment or profit.
Estimated option-price change ≈ delta × stock-price change
Option Greeks on Wikipedia
Implied volatility IV
The annualized move implied by an option's market price. It describes expected magnitude, not direction. Higher IV usually means richer call and put premiums because the market is pricing a wider range of outcomes.
Example: 20% IV on a $100 stock implies roughly a $1.25 one-standard- deviation daily move: $100 × 20% ÷ 16.
There is no universal good IV. Compare the same ticker across expirations and with its own history. High IV can pay sellers more, but it also signals larger expected moves and can make buybacks more expensive.
Rough daily expected move = stock price × IV ÷ √252 ≈ stock price × IV ÷ 16
Theta per day
Estimated one-day change in the option's per-share value from time passing, with stock price, IV, rates, and dividends unchanged. Long options usually show negative theta; that decay generally helps a short-option seller.
Example: theta of −0.05 suggests about $0.05 per share, or $5 per standard contract, of one-day decay under unchanged model inputs.
More negative is faster decay, not free profit. Theta changes and is often strongest for short-dated ATM options, where stock-move risk is also concentrated.
Estimated one-day option change from time ≈ theta per day
Breakeven
The stock price where the combined shares-and-option position is approximately flat at expiration, before taxes. Put breakeven uses strike. Covered-call breakeven uses broker share basis when available, otherwise current stock price.
A breakeven is not a stop-loss and does not cap downside. Below a short put's or covered call's breakeven, stock losses continue dollar for dollar at expiration.
Put breakeven = strike − net credit per share
Covered-call breakeven = share basis − net credit per share
Strike distance
The absolute percentage gap between strike and stock price. It shows cushion size, not direction and not the probability of assignment.
Example: a $95 strike on a $100 stock is 5% away.
Radar's product guide calls 10%+ excellent cushion, 7%+ good, 4%+ fair, and below 4% poor. That is a ranking input, not a safety guarantee. High-IV stocks can cross a wide gap quickly.
Strike distance % = |strike − stock price| ÷ stock price × 100%
Estimated fee
The assumed per-contract charge subtracted from gross credit. Radar currently uses $0.65 for one contract. Your broker's actual commission, regulatory fees, and contract-specific charges may differ.
Lower is better, but spread cost can dwarf the stated fee. Verify both at the broker before ordering.
Radar calculation inputs Model assumptions
Radar assumes a 100-share standard contract, a $0.65 opening fee, a 4.5% risk-free rate, and 0% dividend yield. It estimates delta and theta with Black–Scholes only when IV is available. Quotes, Greeks, and midpoint fills remain estimates.
Model output is useful for comparison, not certainty. Dividends, early assignment, changing IV, stale quotes, and actual fills can move real results away from the estimate.
Simple annualized return = period return × 365 ÷ DTE
Check the contract, then the model.
